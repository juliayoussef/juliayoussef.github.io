tl;dr: Good book, go read — Wheeler-Reed deconstructs the idea of a unique 'Judeo-Christian' set of values, so often invoked and taken for granted in today's political discourse. It evokes anger — anger, because, regardless of one's particular faith, Wheeler-Reed successfully argues that the 'Judeo-Christian' values so often argued to be implemented as law reflect only part of the diverse traditions that informed Christianity; and, to me, perhaps tradition most associated with institutions and the state, rather than some singular theological inheritance. Anyways — this is a review and an essay. I wanted my thoughts down, but the sourcing, as always, could use more rigor. 

----

Anyone who has ever met me in real life has realized I have a slight (only slight) interest in religious history. Specifically, the evolution (!!!) of Christianity from a small, local sect within Second Temple Judaism into an organized, state-level religious behemoth. I like understanding why individuals believe what they do, the contexts in which those beliefs develop, and the ways different contexts drive changes in the manner religion is understood and practiced. I studied classics throughout school, and have a Coptic family background — so, naturally, I ended up as a casual reader of everything relating to Apostolic and Patristic age Christianity, "Gnosticism," pre-Nicene theologies, and local syncretism. 

Likewise, as an American in the United States, subject to its laws and philosophies, I've followed the apparent rise of a particular flavor of Christianity within the Right. There's something uniquely fascinating, to me, to read about the emerging understandings of text and tradition that would transform regional apocalyptic beliefs into a religion of empire and authority; to try to understand that development into the sort of Christianity that exerts such enormous influence on the culture of the nation I live within. There's something uniquely infuriating, too, to read the emerging understandings of text and tradition in the current American political sphere, given their consequences, my understanding of earlier works, and my exposure to other forms of belief. 

That is not to say that beliefs cannot evolve, cannot be reinvented in the context of each period, each time and place. And yet, maybe it is the pedant in me that is so annoyed when the conversation is shaped by a fundamental(ist) assumption that it is only *their* understanding of Christianity that is the "correct" one, the "obvious" one, the one that is evidently so clear to all that to *not* subscribe to their version is to be willfully obtuse. The idea, too, that there is an "objective" zeitgeist of belief that, from Jesus to Paul to ... Billy Graham, forms the heart and drives the law of our Nation, consistent and unchanging. 

Wheeler-Reed's "Regulating Sex in the Roman Empire" essentially adds fuel to the fire of my frustration. Inspired by the current conservative rhetoric of a "return" to "Judeo-Christian values," the work traces evidence of ideologies within early-empire Rome, Judaism, and early Christianity to show not only the variation, but contradiction, in the supposition that there were shared mindsets or coherent "values" towards marriage, sex, and sexuality. In particular, Wheeler-Reed argues that these so-called "Judeo-Christian values" are more reminiscent of Roman law than either Jewish or Christian thought. Not only that, he highlights distinct traditions within a (non-monolithic) Judaism, and foregrounds Christianity's own evolving rhetoric, subject to internal debate, and distinct from older, "biblical" views. Chapters 1 through 4 follow four snapshots of cultural ideologies. Chapters 1 and 2 examine the perspectives embedded in writings of the early Roman Empire and contemporary Judaism. Chapters 3 and 4, then, consider in both contrast and continuation the crystallizing early Christianity and debates within its early church. 

Rome, for instance, is described as having a conception of marriage linked to the empire itself: not quite Plato's 'utopian' state-arranged unions, but nevertheless state-incentivized and state required — a response to the decades of civil instability, blamed on celibacy and singleness by the Roman people and reformers. With the *lex Iulia et Pappia Poppaea*, Wheeler-Reed writes, marriage was a "duty incumbent on all Roman[s]" between particular ages, and without it and offspring, an individual was limited in their available avenues of both political participation and inheritance. As contemporary romance novels like *Leucippe and Clitophon* illustrate, "Pleasure, well-measured and harnessed to the common good, [could be] understood by the ancient not to contradict but to ratify the actions of honorable men and women." (quoted from Cooper, *The Virgin and the Bride*), nevertheless enforcing a state-bound duty to marry and procreate. 

Judaism, like Rome, "privileges the home, marriage, and procreation." Characterized by the commandment to "be fruitful and multiply," Wheeler-Reed presents Jewish writing as placing emphasis on marriage, children, and the family — but unlike Roman thought, it did not likewise emphasize desire as a good within relationships. Rather, to some (Philo of Alexandria), pleasure may even be 'unlawful.' In the story of Tobit, a Jewish man preparing to marry makes a promise to God that it is not lust, but sincere duty, that inspires the marriage (8:4-7). Marriage as an aim and duty in itself is repeatedly affirmed by a number of stories featuring singles guided towards partnership — and, as in Rome,  the basis of family. Philo would go so far as to consider eunuchs (*On the Migration of Abraham* 69) and marriage with barren women (*On the Special Laws* 3.34) condemnable. To him, marriage and procreationism are a prophylactic against the evil of desire. However, procreative philosophies were not universally adhered to, nor applied in every context. Some later authors (such as that of the Jubilees, and Josephus) downplay the commandment to multiply, and temporary asceticism was understood in the priesthood and through Nazirite vows. Groups like the Essenes were said to have not married at all: contemporary descriptions and their own works indicate that abstinence was maintained for the sake of ritual purity, and perhaps avoidance of women, believed to be "more easily overcome by the spirit of promiscuity than are men," (*Testament of Reuben* 5:3). While little can be said about the everyday Jewish experience from these texts alone, Wheeler-Reed notes that "much of this literature ... reduces women's bodies to the function of reproduction," (pg. 61).

Christianity emerged as a local messianic movement within Second Temple Judaism, beginning under the heavy shadow of Rome before developing into a religion of empire. The presence of contradictory perspectives on marriage and the family as Christianity institutionalized reflects the challenge of developing as distinct theology within and from Jewish, Hellenistic, and Roman philosophies. Wheeler-Reed presents two modes of thought competing to define orthodoxy, and it is this debate, to me, that positions his work so concretely within the current political moment: one he characterizes as "antifamily, antimarriage, antihousehold, antiprocreation" and the other, its opposite. 

Consider the writings of Paul, preaching in the immediate decades post-Christ, where followers believed his coming would be within their lifetimes. "Are you bound to a wife? Do not seek to be free. Are you free from a wife? Do not seek a wife," (1 Corinthians 7:27). It is better to be single, though "it is better to marry than to burn with passion," (v.9), as he says in 1 Thessalonians, each ought to "control his own vessel in holiness and honor, not in the passion of desire, like gentiles who do not know God," (4:3-7). Contrary to what would be expected in discussions of marriage by an educated Jewish man, Paul does not mention children in this discussion; Wheeler-Reed suggests that is precisely *because* procreation is no longer viewed as marriage's purpose. The gospels themselves say little on the purpose of marriage — though, in a discussion of divorce, Matthew describes "those who choose to live like eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven. The one who can accept this should accept it." (19:12). In the later non-canonical *Acts of Paul and Thecla*, the titular Thecla refuses her fiancé and swears herself to celibacy, and through her righteousness becomes capable not only of preaching but also of baptism in the name of Jesus. Christian hagiographies also emphasize chastity: consider the traditional story of Saint Demiana, a daughter of a third-century Egyptian governor said to have chosen a life devoted to Christ by rejecting marriage, and was martyred for it. Antifamily ideologies within early Christianity appear more permissive of female abstinence and choice than those in Second Temple Jewish or Roman contexts — seemingly allowing room for a novel kind of ascetic sexual ethic. After all, "There is neither Jew nor Gentile, neither slave nor free, nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus," (Galatians 3:28) allows space for more egalitarian perspectives. 

However, though "...of that day and hour [of Jesus' coming] no one knows," (Matthew 24:36), it became increasingly clear the Second Coming would not be "now." It is thus not surprising that communities began to concern themselves with a philosophy of the future — and the family. Take, for instance, the oft-quoted: "Wives, submit yourselves to your own husbands as you do to the Lord," (Ephesians 5:22). Ephesians appears to concern itself with the family as a unit for Christ-like living, for the Church and *not* the polity. Additionally, it mentions and addresses children themselves. While there was not quite a supposition of equality in Corinthians nor Thessalonians, Ephesians emphasizes the subservient role of the woman to the man — a topic taken even farther in the Pastoral Epistles (1& 2 Timothy, Titus). Consider "I do not permit a woman to teach or assume authority over a man; she must be quiet," 1 Timothy 2:12, and the prescription of 1 Timothy to women to provide children for the church (2:15). Men are positioned as authorities within the household, and the Church as authority over them. 

Critically, despite their attribution to Paul, these ideologies are thought to have been written by different authors, with the Pastorals (almost certainly) and Ephesians (less confidently) potentially written by someone other than the author of Galatians, Romans, Corinthians, or Thessalonians, with the *Acts of Paul and Thecla* written by someone else entirely. In the period just after Jesus' death, and through the consolidation of the Church, there appears to have been significant debate between the optimal Christian ethic: eternal chastity, procreative marriage, and the continuum between or coexistence of both. It is clear to see, then, how both reproductive philosophies and monastic traditions could emerge from the same textual history. Equally clear, then, that not only is there not one unified "Christian" perspective, but that there is certainly neither one united "Judeo-Christian" one. As the Church constructed itself, as it became integrated into the political structure of Empire, defining itself relative to heresy, it would find room for both philosophies. But, as Wheeler-Reed presents, with extensive discourse church fathers would declare celibacy the 'more Christian' life. After the 'Jovinian controversy' of the late 4th century, this would be made explicit: marriage and procreationism are not co-equal to ascetic practice — it is *chastity* that is rewarded, prioritized, and valued foremost. 

Both the introduction and final chapter emphasize that Wheeler-Reed is not trying to produce a "total history," per se; rather, a general one, that "concentrates ... on describing differences, transformations, continuities, mutations, and so forth." (pg. 105). Attempting to be more of a "map-maker" than a "historian," he traces the ways power over individuals drives discourses concerning the bounds of sex, marriage, and procreation. By noting the relationship between marriage and the transference of wealth and property, he acknowledges plainly the relationship between marriage and politics. Consequently, while the first four chapters in this work focus on the prescriptions towards marriage and sexuality in antiquity, it is the fifth that makes clear that the dynamic between power and sexual control is still a factor in the modern era. Further, Wheeler-Reed seeks to "unmask" the "modern forms of power that use Christianity in the service of policing modern society in the United States." To, as he quotes from Clark's "Antifamilial Tendencies in Ancient Christianity," "push and jab" at the contemporary rhetoric and "make manifest the ways in which their authors seek to present their highly constructed arguments as 'natural' interpretations, obvious to all 'rational' people." 

This post began just before I finished reading this last chapter -- the structural similarity of my beginning statements and Clark's formulation is perhaps an indication of a widespread sentiment. On one hand, it is possible reading such a work that indulges a perspective I already had, in part, was dangerous — validating underlying, and not fully verified, perspectives. On the other hand, however, I find that Wheeler-Reed was successful in his aim of being a "map-maker," providing a defined view of the discourse I myself only had a rough outline of. I've enjoyed the direct, almost casual way that he notes the presence of distinct and competing ideologies, though to some extent I wanted more. The book reads as if its author has so thoroughly understood his sources and his positions that the evidence presented is almost more an outline than a robust investigation (perhaps the very "push[ing] and jab[bing]" he means) — as if the points were more self-evident than I, as a reader, interpreted them to be. Nevertheless, I found it a good read, and supplemented with comparable discussions in works like *Dirt, Shame, and Status: Perspectives on Same-Sex Sexuality in the Bible and the Ancient World* by Thomas Kazen, which provided a more targeted investigation of a characteristic ancient sexual ethic: what were appropriate, and inappropriate, manners of sexuality?

Wheeler-Reed's last chapter is not merely an exposition of his approach to the work; rather, it is also a critical examination of the ways *We* — the United States, not Rome, not Judea, not the New Testament and Early Church — balance political incentives, religious belief, and prescriptions towards marriage and its function. He traces distinct perspectives towards marriage within the current-day discourse, particularly the outcry of "family values" in light of ~recent nationwide legalization of same-sex marriage, efforts to outlaw abortion and contraceptives, and pushes towards a focus on the household. Most interestingly, Wheeler-Reed engages with the notion that it is these "Judeo-Christian Family Values," (trademark that and throw it on a bumper sticker) are representative of *societal goods*. This is the position argued by organizations like the Heritage Foundation, and other groups that purport to embrace "facts not feelings" style rationality who nevertheless present panaceas more in alignment with their own religious interpretations than would be expected of independent logic. 

In fact, in an article called "The Wealth of Nations Depends on the Health of Familes," Patrick Fagan makes a loose economic argument that the economy (re: the state, the future, the patriotic duty to capital) is served best by monogamous, conjugal, reproductive marriages between men and women, who never divorce, and who feature wives who have never had any prior partners, and who raise children who will then thrive within the market having learned from the model of the family. It is remarkable, to me, how the evidently optimal social arrangements for our 21st century economy and polity happen to align with the religious proscriptions Fagan was, by chance, born into, and supposedly created by a society two thousand years prior with a fundamentally different social world. But, as Wheeler-Reed notes, it isn't early Christianity these beliefs resemble, but rather the patriotic laws of Rome. Consider, "Not only does [the traditional marriage] produce the greatest average human capital for the marketplace; it also produces the best citizens for the state and the common good." It is a challenge to summarize Wheeler-Reed's final chapter adequately —  I recommend reading it yourself, as it is both the most witty and the most damning towards figures purporting to speak with the authority of 'rationality' or some idealized, early, 'original' Christianity. 

His most effective point, however, is in his questions. His work, and others that cover similar topics, present a compelling case for the diversity in philosophies regarding the 'purpose' of marriage in the ancient world. This does not even fully take into consideration of the variety of perspectives we have today — that marriage is an expression of love, or of mutual support. In doing so, he (and consequently, we) are forced to ask *why* prominent conservative groups arguing from Christian principles *only* ever seem to prioritize the procreative marriage ethic. There are a number of ways early Christian thought (if we are to claim we are abiding by "original" tradition) could be naively applied to law. Wheeler-Reed includes examples that incentivize singleness, chastity within marriage, or even state-demanded unions. Ought the state to penalize marriages that suffer from infertility? Ban weddings between post-procreative couples? Should any sort of *porneia* be condemned, be punished? While chastity-before-marriage is a common conservative anthem, it carries with it the implication of *before marriage*. Where, in the right-wing discourses and GOP conventions, are citizens encouraged to remain single and part of their communities? Where are the exaltations of those who reject marriage and the household? When we discuss "looksmaxxing" trends, and political wives driven to converge on an increasingly homogeneous beauty ideal, and the president (~2026) regularly comments on women's appearances, where is the condemnation of desire itself? 

The sexual ethic articulated by the Paul of Corinthians, Galatians, and Thessalonians, and the one advocated by the Pastoral Epistles, both belong to the historical inheritance of Christian thought; but, of the two, it was the former that prioritized over the latter by Church Fathers, and the former that is not discussed in the political debates. The option to not marry; the option to marry, and not have children. And yet, it is the procreative tradition that we see. The one that, like earlier instances of pro-family ideology, reinforces hierarchy: hierarchy between the church/state and the household, and within the household, the hierarchy between the man, father, husband and the woman, mother, wife. Why, in this encouragement of tradition, of family, of 'natural' and 'clear' biblical interpretation, we do not see an equally ardent push towards monasticism? How convenient, that the current Christian Nationalist tradition emerged from a Protestant sect that, unlike Catholicism or Orthodoxy, participates far less in such practices? How funny, too, that of the aspects of procreationist thought that are pitched as Judeo-Christian, it is *not* Paul's, nor Philo's, where desire is pushed aside for the sake of familial and pious duty, but rather that of Augustus? Where desire is privileged when used for the sake of the empire and the state? 

Despite my interests, I myself am merely culturally, not faithfully, Christian. I have no state in re-interpreting and re-inventing Christian theology for my own beliefs. But, with its use in politics, I find myself frustrated at the values and ideologies taken as a given as "correct," especially when they are then used to impact the lives of millions. I do not pretend that every political actor is sincere; I cannot pretend that I don't know that people can lie, or use religion to strengthen their position or further their goals. But, as with the rise creationist discourse, it feels worse when the arguments used are not self-consistent, and calls to knowledge and authority reveal nothing more than the ignorance of the nation's leaders. 

(I do find it funny, though — the invocation of end-times rhetoric within the political Right's base concurrent with the encouragement to reward having many children)
