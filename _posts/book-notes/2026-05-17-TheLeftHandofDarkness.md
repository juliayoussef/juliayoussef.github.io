*The Left Hand of Darkness* is an excellent work, definitely recommend. I don't spoil it, here, though, so feel free to read on regardless if you have read it or not. 

These were some of my thoughts, thinking through the question posed by Le Guin: what is the relationship between gender, hierarchy, and war? These notes see me thinking through that question, referencing other works and other species. I try to consider what removing one of the most prevalent forms of hierarchy in humans may mean for our forms of conflict. 

tl;dr: I suspect war would happen anyways, as long as there is Othering, as long as there is scarcity. 

Reader, enjoy!

I (not-so-recently) finished Ursula Le Guin's *The Left Hand of Darkness*, slowly at first, then all at once. I was recommended it online, I think, based on some other works I've come to enjoy, namely the *Terra Ignota* and *Children of Time*. Having only read pieces of *Earthsea* as a kid, and "The Ones who Walk Away from Omelas," years ago, I had no real sense of Le Guin's writing. I suppose I understand now why she's so recommended. 

Part of the Hainish Cycle (which I haven't (yet!) read anything else from), the book is set in a universe where humans are distributed across 89 or so planets, many of which are loosely aligned in the Ekumen (a sort of committee/trades-council sort-of-governmental system). With fast-as-light speed and faster-than-light communication, such loose affiliations may represent the best-case most-feasible interstellar government. Its a system that, in hindsight, clearly inspired later works, including Card's Hundred Worlds. Seeing the word "ansible" was like being struck by lightning -- I had no idea this was where the term originated, now that its so ubiquitous in the language of sci-fi. 

That's not the point, I suppose. The background lore is not the most interesting thing in this work. A representative from the Ekumen (from our world-equivalent, Terra) is sent to Winter, where the newly-encountered individuals are human (properly so -- persons *and* human, *främling* in Valentine's Hierarchy of Foreignness). However -- there is one fundamental difference: the humans on Winter, or Gethen as they call it, are not gendered. Not only do they lack physical markers of sex, outside of a kind of estrus known as kemmer, they do not appear to have any social gendered roles either. Anyone can sire a child, and likewise, anyone can bear one, a hermaphroditic existence only relevant a few days per month. Sex exists only as a potential, gender a temporary state, and as a perpetual binary only as a foreign concept. The world, then, is Le Guin's exploration of that absence's consequences. 

I am not surprised this was recommended after *Too Like the Lightning* and *Children of Time*. I hope to, in a proper post, dissect each's approach to gender more in depth. *Terra Ignota*, by (UChicago's own!) Dr. Ada Palmar, features a 'post-gender' society (in the same was we are a 'post-racial' society, that is, not at all). In a Reddit AMA, she describes it as "a future which has attempted to achieve equality through silencing and ignoring gender expression." Despite the ubiquity of "theys" and "theirs," and the work's internal refutation that the gender binary is inherent to human thought, it admits that humans society has been structured around gendered signals for so long that the abrupt taboo of gender-discussion is insufficient. *Terra Ignota* does not exist without gender, but it ignores the issue, declaring the problem of sexism to be "finished" and "done" too early. Here, *Terra Ignota* cannot escape gender -- humans are still sexed, culture, language, and society still steeped in dichotomous language and orientations of power baked with gendered thought. 

Children of Time, on the other hand, represents an almost-inversion of our own dynamics. Exploring gender from the perspective of a sentient, sapient *non-human* species (*ramen*, Valentine?) of uplifted *Portia* jumping spiders, the reader is confronted with non-primate, non-vertebrate social dynamics. The work tacks generations of male-female spider pairs as their society technologically innovates, becomes more collaborative, and evolves towards a sort of egalitarianism. But the specific traits associated with "male" or "female," the spider-masculine and spider-feminine, are different than our own...the starting state of gendered relations in jumping spiders was fundamentally violent in a way ours never was: jumping spider females eat males after mating. While it becomes more egregious to them as they rapidly evolve into social intelligence, it is a history that cannot be ignored. Males are forced into more subservient roles, exploited, always aware of their position. They are both physically and socially in weaker positions than the females -- paralleling our own feminine-associated traits. In a sense, Children of Mind, to me, is able to illustrate how arbitrary the constellation of traits called masculine and feminine can be, in their specific association with male and female. The inversion works because it is able to contextualize gender and associated traits through the dynamic between the gendered-class with power, and the one without. 

Both represent different social structures, both question our engagement with the concepts, yet neither can escape the friction. But, on Winter, or Gethen, they never had gender at all. How do you associate sets of dichotomous values, beliefs, traits, etc. without a dichotomy in the population? How do you have systemic mistreatment of a childbearing class if *any* individual is equally likely to bear a child? *The Left Hand of Darkness* is meant literally -- its a snippet from an in-world poem. Light is the left hand of darkness; darkness the right hand of light. It is a phrase directly compared to yin and yang by the offworlder, the Envoy; indicating convergent thinking towards dualist philosophy. Dichotomous thinking, then, exists, set on equal ground, yet individuals themselves seem freed from the dualism of sex. 

The social patterns in *Terra Ignota* and *Children of Time* are familiar -- despite the foreignness of the future, or of the different species, there is competition, flirtation, conflict, performance, and war. But Gethen is different. While there is social ... conflict (kingdoms, and a concept similar-to-but-not-exactly-like "losing face"), it presents in a distinct manner. The novel was written in 1969, in the throws of human exceptionalism. Le Guin's father was an anthropologist, and that field's ever-present questioning of the ubiquity of human experiences bleeds through. Genderless, Gethen has no seduction (without exceptionally good timing), no perpetual sexual tension, no sense of power implicit in sexual dimorphism. There is social power, political power, but there is no war, there is no rape, of people or nature. Le Guin notes, through the Envoy, the representative, Genly Ai, that man is the only being who wages war, who uses violence so cruelly. In a world without gender, without the conqueror and the conquered, without the pillager and the pillaged, without the man and the woman, Ai suggests there may be no War. 

While it can be difficult to know where an Author's opinion ends and a Character's begins, Ai himself is a misogynist, something I took too long to realize. It is he who thinks of gender most strongly in binary terms, hierarchial terms. The question, though, reads as a part of the larger philosophical theme of the work: to what extent can gender and societal inequities be disentangled? The question is there: Gethen has no gender. Gethen has no war. Are they related? I sometimes wonder if the same underlying ideas of mid-century human exceptionalism suggests that just as we are uniquely capable of specific good or ability: language, tool-use, religion .... we are also uniquely capable of specific evils: war, genocide, cruelty. In saying we are the only ones who war, we suggest it may be something fundamental to man. But perhaps, that is also what lets us imagine a world *without* war. That there are means of living and society that can be imagined without it. 

In 1974, Jane Goodall observed two chimpanzee groups in Gombe, Tanzania, wage what traditionally can only be described in terms of war. More than mere intimidation, more than simply interpersonal conflict, once-allied individuals were ambushed and beaten to near-death by raids. Goodall describes in her work, *Through a Window: My thirty Years with the Chimpanzees of Gombe,* true violence: "Satan [one of the apes], cupping his hand below Sniff's [another chimp] chin to drink the blood that welled from a great wound on his face." The exact nature of this specific conflict is uncertain -- as far as I am aware, it may have been political tension gone awry, different sides forming around males competing for dominance. When I first read Ai muse on the very-human nature of war, this is what I though of. Chimps on patrols. Chimps committing infanticide. Chimps waging war, for territory, hierarchy, resources. We are not the only creatures who commit cruelty, not the only ones who war. 

(When did triplets in writing become so evocative of AI?)

But was that a good comparison? Are they not, just like us, hierarchical primates? Chimps, just like us, have social roles according to their understood sex. Even more so than us, maybe: they have alpha males, they have inherited rank, they have established practices of dominance and submission. In many ways, too, they are *more* sexually dimorphic than us (men, where are your display canines?!). And, as chimpanzees and many other primates demonstrate, coercive sex is not restricted to *Homo sapiens*. Rank determines freedom: who gets access to food, to mating opportunities, to safety? Even as non-human examples, what difference does it make, Ai could easily ask, if they are just as gendered?

There are notable cases, though, where primates do not behave so violently, despite similar underlying sexual morphology. In the mid 1980's, half the males of a troop of baboons died from tuberculosis -- in fact, the most aggressive of said males. Their choice food happened to be a garbage dump -- one with an outbreak of bovine TB. Suddenly, the ratio of females to males doubled. Because male baboons leave the troops they are born in, eventually, many of the surviving would leave. Remarkably, the behavior of the troop was perpetually changed. Lesser-rank males were less subject to harassment from higher ranking ones; aggression, when it occurred, was more likely to occur between ~equals. Notable, aggression was much less likely to happen towards females. Stress-indicators in low-ranking males and females were essentially absent when contrasted with other troops. And, over a decade after the deaths, this distinct social style persisted -- intergenerational transfer of a 'healthier' social culture. This was not war -- rather, inter-group violence -- but suggests as females gained social power, relations between genders, at least, eased. It was not as significant a factor in the rank-based social hierarchy. Bonobos are also interesting, and represent more of a species-wide sample. Despite the recent~ split from the sister species, chimpanzees, they display remarkably different social organization: matriarchal, with social disagreements more often resolves with sex than gang-style aggression. Recent studies have brought evidence against their reputation as peaceful apes --males, apparently, exhibit *more* aggression towards one another than male chimps -- but they nonetheless do not exhibit the same war-like traits. As one ape researcher put it, "Chimpanzees murder, and bonobos don't." 

*Children of Time* would indicate that as long as there are differences in the social power of genders, it does not particularly matter if its patriarchal or matriarchal -- gendered violence and binary social organization still occur. The rise in social power in the baboons and bonobos, though, saw relative reductions in individual stress, cruelty, and intergender aggression. Why? The relationship between female social power against the still-relevant physical power of the males? Perhaps its that balancing of modes of power each gender can hold through the complex interaction of physicality, inherited rank, and understood role -- rather than the juxtaposition of "oppressor" and "oppressed" alone. Systemic violence between genders, and against other groups(?), in these specific examples, appears to decrease without the systemic othering that occurs (inherently? as a result of?) strong power imbalances between genders. Extrapolating (dangerous! two examples should *not* generalize, but can prompt thought), one may ask, if that gendered dynamic were reduced, as on Winter, would there be war at all?  Or, so minimal a degree as to be categorically absent?

I find it difficult to believe there would be no other instances of "us" vs. "them" to serve as a model. But what else is so present, in every culture, region, population, as gender? It is difficult to even consider a conflict or instance of othering in today's world that *isn't* framed in language associated with masculinity and femininity. The strong takes the weak, the conqueror the conquered, the dominant the submissive, etc. But, how often is human social structure so reductive that all power is in the hands of one gender, and one gender only? History has seen thousands of different approaches towards either egalitarianism or the disempowerment of a gendered class. Universally, though, humans war, and to a greater extent than any other vertebrate species. And, we do so with, perhaps, less of a sexual dichotomy than other primates. 

Gethen is not without nationalism, not without conflict, not without social and cultural taboos. The genderlessness of its inhabitants is twofold: not only are they hermaphoditic, but also, for the majority of the time, they are not even capable of reproduction. If only the former - seduction would still exist, the perpetual tension, the underlying motivator would still have a social role. If only the latter, there would still be as much gendered rules as seen in apes that go through estrus. It is the combination that produces such a unique society. Gender is removed as the axes of social hierarchy, or of otherness, but there is still tribalism, there is still rank. 

Prior to the arrival of the Envoy, Gethen saw two regions developing into nationhood: the Ortega, with their bureaucracy and secret police, pushing towards "modernity," and Karhide, a kingdom encouraging patriotism in response. I cannot take Genly Ai's perspective on the basis of war as Le Guin's. Ai is insecure, uncomfortable with the genderless nature of the aliens he finds himself surrounded by. He calls those around him "he," the neutral he, the potential he, but he nonetheless -- save for his 'landlady,' or those he feels exhibits feminine traits. When an individual he has assigned masculine exhibits 'feminine' traits, he is disgusted. It takes his friend, his love, his advisor entering an early stage of kemmer for Ai to realize that this person was as much "she" as they were "he," -- both and neither. Ai finds himself constantly in a kind of masculine-hurt, fluffing up his chest, his manhood threatened, by a culture that has no concept of manhood, no concept of 'feminization,' no paradigm around one half of the human race exhibiting some fundamentally inferior set of traits. What does he know of war, and gender? To suppose there is no war, as there are no men? Yet he sees the nationstate emerging, he sees a planet with limited resources: can there not be war? Can there not be cruelty nonetheless?

The humans on Gethen are not native to it. How could they be? It is cold, barren, and isolating. It is bereft of many of the Eden-like blessings that Earth has (mangos, chickpeas, pita bread; forests, jungles, coral reefs; things-that-look-like-us-and-remind-us-we-are-not-alone). Genly Ai notes that it is incredible they unraveled evolution all on their own: alien transplants, the humans are unrelated to any of the organisms on the planet. There are no mammals, vertebrates, amniote-animals like us. Everything is different. On Earth, the most-common, most-universal form of othering in humans is not, I think, gender. It is the difference between us, and everything else. There is human vs. non-human. Human and animal. Person-worth-respect, and non-person-worth-exploitation. Hierarchy has just as often emerged through this binary, as well, especially when the not-human is actually quite similar. Sometimes the greatest acts of hate and dehumanization seemed aimed at those almost-like-us, other peoples we deem not-quite-human -- whereas destructive disregard is aimed towards those not-like-us-at-all. We wage war with the 'inferior' people group of our choice -- not nematodes, not fish, not rocks.
 
Le Guin has created a world where the non-human is so different as to be disregarded, except when needed as food or in regional ecologies. Humans are simply incomparable to the wildlife. As for Nature, it itself is brutal, cold, an ice-age planet with extensive glaciation. Does it all make it harder to call other humans non-human? When, they are all so clearly more similar to one another than the rest of the world? When they are all fighting the same Nature just to survive? If, on Earth, the most universal hierarchies are one gender over another, Mankind over Nature, hierarchies that model conquest and war, then what can Gethen have? Humans are a immigrant community surviving the storm. Is it that which brings unity? 

Nevertheless, as stated, they move towards some type of conflict and escalation; perhaps, even, war. Technology has progressed to make Winter bearable; Nature no longer the universal oppressor. Nation-states coalesce and expand. Man has gained a semblance of control over ravaging-winter, formed political hierarchy, cultivated regional identity distinct from the Other. Maybe, at sufficient complexity, it is unavoidable. Perhaps Le Guin's answer is that gender simply removes one mode of competition, one mode of inspiring war. The book forced me to ask if there be dichotomous notions of power and 'natural right' to oppress without gender, and I think there can be. As long as there is hierarchy, I think, as long as there's competition: there may be impetus for war. As long as there is scarcity of resources, the means of fighting for them, and an Enemy, and Other, and Us and a Them, I think culture can bring about war. We are, after all, hierarchical primates. The manner, extent, and degree may be all that differs. It is only the specifics of Gethen that have not brought them there yet. 

Maybe, Genly Ai, its not gender that begets war, but war itself that teaches us to organize instances of difference into hierarchies of conqueror and conquered -- gender included.

I am reminded of the diversity of war. War with loss of face, not life. War with kidnapping, not killing. War with territorial gains, cruelty, hostages, but not rape, not arson, not destruction. Gender may be entirely divorced from war, masculinity and femininity's collections of juxtaposing traits entirely orthogonal to that of conflict. 

But, hey, I don't know though. 

If you got this far, reader, its a good book. Worth reading. The imagery is beautiful. The world feels lived in, tiny details and background characters bringing life to the alien setting. Its beautiful, and thoughtful, and ... in classic sci-fi style, forces us to ask questions about our own society through the hypothetical. Anyways.
