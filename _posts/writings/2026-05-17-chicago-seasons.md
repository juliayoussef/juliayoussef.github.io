It is now officially Spring (though, it has been since mid-March, and truly, its borderline summer). The cherry blossoms at Jackson Park have bloomed; the lake sees the odd swimmer; divvy bike stations near my apartment find themselves conspicuously empty. 

I don't often walk past the university quad -- it's not on my way to work, or the lab -- but when I do, it feels like it has bloomed as much as the flowers have. Not that the flowers on campus, outside some bulbs, really bloom - petunias and lettuce and evergreen branches are deposited seasonally in order to maintain the aesthetic, of course. In Spring, though, the quad becomes a community space, full of students, professors, and the odd dog. It brings me more memories of college than nearly anything else - and nearly exclusively positive ones. While study spaces themselves come with the baggage of the stress felt during the studying, the quad in Springtime just reminds me of early-season, end-of-year optimism. It feels like picnics and expression and anticipation of what will come next. 

I used to carry a throw blanket around in my backpack all day just for the grass. I'd lay on the quad like a stereotype -- stretched out on my stomach, (text)book (err - kindle or laptop) in front of me, reading. Textbooks are heavy, and computers overheat. I wanted old classics. I wanted magazines. Code doesn't work so well without WiFi if you need GitHub or GoogleCollab. I was envious of the artists and humanities majors around me. But, for moments, before the battery died, when I had papers to read, I could pretend. 

In hindsight, I'm sure my friends and I made the perfect university photo-op. There was me, QuestBridge, pale with curly-hair, dressed like Jurassic Park's Dr. Sattler. Some of my friends included:
- a jewish-iranian non-binary lesbian
- a psych/music major with sometimes-white hair and a jet-black aesthetic
- a genderqueer west african immigrant with bleached eyebrows and a penchant for reptiles with sweet names who was, perhaps, the most community-driven person I have ever known. 
- a fellow Odyssey Scholar, a Belarussian math-major who nevertheless who could go toe-to-toe with even the most online on internet memes and HouseMD lore. 

I wonder if we looked the picture of a University ad. We had our thrifted leather jackets, our flowing skirts, patchwork tattoos. Our reusable silverware, our tote bags. If we all met up, in Spring, would the University ask for a photo? Would a picture of us, "diversity" embodied, with Phil the Phoenix, the mascot, have been able to make-up for the protests and sit-ins? Would it have meant the University didn't let the CPD into the dorms, and arrest the one who was once my friend from their room, for involvement in the pro-Palestine protests? I don't know. But I am reminded of, in Fall, welcoming first years in, they begged another RA to pose for the photos with their Odyssey Scholar shirt. 

Spring is full of life. The world seems to awaken from its misery - the trees' leaves rapidly develop and their delicate shade returns to Hyde Park. Maple seeds flutter across the town, a headstart before the cottonwood blanket the neighborhood in summer-snow. I've been here 5 years, now. I've come to recognize the order in which the flowers sprout around campus. The daffodils, the snowbells. The lilies of the valley, the tulips, and the allium. July and august will see the true lilies fiery entrance, tiger's and yellows. Milkweed and catmint are common - native plants present enough to see pollinators in the summer. This is the time when the university likes to rapidly-replace any of the plants that show the slightest sign of weakening. And every year, the grass that lines the pathways are removed and replaced with new sod. My friends and I ventured a guess, once, that the grass replacement cost about $100k. One full student's tuition and housing costs went to the replacement of grass. I knew a friend who's tuition payment was made using a parent's life insurance payout. I can't help but notice the grass.

Spring is also full of stress. There is only a week between the end of the Winter Quarter and the start of Spring. The final sprint feels like a marathon. You must finish your work. Must balance your tasks. You can sleep when you're dead (when the quarter ends). You can recover later (when the quarter ends). The primary campus library, the Reg, used to be 24 hours pre-COVID. Now, though, in the last weeks of the quarter, it will stay open till 4am. Caffeine vesicles (energy drinks and coffee cups) overfill the trashcans until mice can be seen to scatter about. Whiteboards caked in ink and cubicles packed - not a single space available to work unless you stake your claim early. I remember all-nighters spent in study rooms, because returning to my dorm meant falling asleep. I remember scheduling my time down to the half-hour. You start to perform a cost-benefit analysis on the cost of caffeine. The cost of sleep. The cost of a late assignment. Failing a class may mean an extra quarter - how much will that cost? A bad grade may determine your future - med school, grad school, law school commitees peering at your GPA, a frown on their face. What will your summer look like? Where will you go? What if you can't go home? What if you don't have a job? What if you have to do some unpaid internship for the sake of your career? How will you move without a car? Without your parents flying in? Where will your stuff go? I, historically, have been very lucky to receive funding each summer. I have stayed in Chicago, because it makes it easier to maintain my things, to move in and out with each school year. 

Spring, unlike my home-state Maryland, which seems to move from winter to summer in an instant, is a real season. With the Spring, comes rains - April showers in the form of vicious thunderstorms that soak the quad (and ruin those multicultural picnic). My family rarely can fly out, rarely can take off work just to help me move. So, every year, I moved all my things in the dorm carts across town to my new place. I generally like the rains - but drizzly mornings as I try to carry my entire life up a walk-up is not the fondest memory. 

This all creates an interesting atmosphere. I remember climbing onto the roof of IHouse, creeping through a window to scamper along the tiles. I remember a 1am summons to play on the local playground: future lawyers, scientists, doctors playing tag in the cool Spring-time evening. Then, waking up in the morning to do my readings. I remember (in disbelief) hearing about 800$ sushi dinners and 1000$ poker bets in the dorm lounges. I remember silly drinking games and quickly-written last-minute essays. The temperate air gives a level of freedom that Winter never can - the intensity of the end of the quarter, end of the year, balanced by the community and life that hesitantly, and then all at once, leaves their hovels of hibernation (dorm rooms) to explore the campus that has emerged from the snow.

I miss those periods, just as I miss my friends, and that stage of life. This is my fifth Spring: I am no longer a student. At the same time, it was a stage that felt...false. Decorated buildings and repotted flowers hid the underlying financial inequality and performative nature of the season. Ever-replaced flowers hid the genuine exhaustion and worry that seemed to plague us all. How many residents would come to me in Spring? How many first-years did I talk to about anxiety? I am no longer an undergrad, no longer an RA. I am in that purgatory between undergrad and graduate school where a million choices lay before me. The figs are just starting to develop on the branches before me. No choices have been yet avoided, no fruit rotting and fallen. I feel alive. I don't hear about, don't experience those problems to the same degree. Maybe its freeing -- but maybe it also allows nostalgia and rose-colored glasses to paint the memories as more pleasent then they were.
